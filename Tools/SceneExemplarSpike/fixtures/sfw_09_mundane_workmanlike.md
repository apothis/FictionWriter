He cleaned out his desk after lunch. He hadn't brought a box. Sandra in HR had given him one of the printer-paper boxes, the kind with the lid that fits over the top, and he carried it back to his cubicle and started in.

The pictures of the kids went in first. Then the small clay frog Jenny had made him in second grade, which had sat on his monitor for nine years. Then the pens. He had a lot of pens. He had thought, at first, that he would leave the pens; they were company property in some technical sense. But Mike from accounting had told him last week that nobody cared about the pens. He took the pens.

The mug. The little USB hub. The phone charger. The Allen wrench he had used, once, four years ago, to tighten the chair. He had kept it in the drawer because you never knew.

He sat down for a minute. He looked at the box. It was not full. After nine years, a printer-paper box was not full.

Mike came by. Mike didn't say anything for a moment. He just stood at the entrance to the cubicle with his hands in his pockets.

"You want to get a beer," Mike said. It wasn't a question.

"Yeah," he said. "Yeah, I'll get a beer."

"O'Malley's at six."

"Okay."

Mike went back to his own cubicle.

He stood up. He looked at the desk one more time. There was nothing on it now except the monitor and the keyboard and the company phone, which they had told him to leave. The desk looked like every other desk on the floor. It looked like a desk you could come in on a Monday and start working at and nobody would know it had ever been his.

He picked up the box. He carried it to the elevator. The girl from marketing was already in the elevator. She looked at the box and then at his face and looked away.

"Sorry," she said.

He didn't say anything. The doors closed.

He took her hand as the door closed behind them. The room was full of the late light through the curtain and she did not switch on the lamp. She wanted the dim. She wanted the world to soften before she would let it touch her.

He stood at the window and she came to him there and he turned and saw her face for what it was: open, willing, not yet undone. He held her at the waist. She did not speak. Words had become a kind of distance between them, and they had agreed, by long silence, to put distance away tonight.

He kissed her at the temple. He kissed her at the throat. She put her hands at the nape of his neck and did not press. She let him lead. She had not, in a long time, let anyone lead, and the relief of it moved through her like water coming back to a riverbed in spring.

He undid the small buttons. He was patient with them. There were many. She watched his hands and thought how, of all the things she had loved about him in their twenty years, it was his hands she had loved best, the careful slow particular attention of them, as though every small fastening were itself a question worth asking properly.

He lifted her. He laid her down. He did not speak either. The light at the window was the colour of honey now. She turned her face toward it. He came to her and she opened to him and they were together, finally, after all the years and after all the silence and after all the small careful refusals of the body that had grown between them like a slow garden of stones.

She wept, after. Not sadly. Not happily either. The way one weeps at the end of a long walk through a country one had thought one would never see again.

He held her. He did not say *don't cry*, because he was a man who had learned the wisdom of letting weather pass through a person without being named.

The light dimmed. She breathed against his collarbone.

"There," she said.

He kissed the top of her head.

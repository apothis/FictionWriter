They had three hours before the train. The hotel was a small one near the station and the room had a single window and the window looked out at the platform where, in the morning, he would board.

She had not told her mother she was here. She had not told anyone.

He drew the curtain. He came to her. She had thought, in the weeks before, that she would say a great deal when this moment came, that she would tell him everything, all the things she had hoarded for the war, but in the event she said nothing. She put her face against his collar. She smelled the wool and the soap and the small foreign smell of the uniform. She did not weep.

He unfastened the back of her dress with hands that were steadier than she had expected they would be. She turned her face into the side of his neck. He kissed her temple. He kissed her brow. He laid her down on the narrow bed and the bed creaked under them and the sound of it was a thing she would remember for the rest of her life: the small foolish creak of a hotel bed near a station, on the last afternoon before the war took him.

He moved over her with a care that astonished her. She had not known a man could be careful in this way. She let him be careful. She gave him what she had. He took it gratefully, as a thirsty man takes water, and she felt herself emptied into him and refilled with the same motion, and the small dim room was for a long while the entire world.

After, they lay in the half-light without speaking. The room had grown dim. She did not want the lamp.

"Don't say it," she said, when she felt him beginning to say it.

He did not say it.

She put her face against his shoulder and let the three hours pass.

It was not, perhaps, the most opportune of moments at which to be admitted into the drawing-room of Aunt Cordelia's house in Devonshire, for the late afternoon had already deepened into that hour, peculiar to the English autumn, when the windows hold less light than the lamps, and the family had assembled, with that quietness which in such houses always denotes the gravest possible conversations, around the fire — and yet I was, after a moment's brief hesitation at the threshold, ushered in by old Bagshawe with the air of one who, having long since exhausted his store of opinions upon the affairs of the household, no longer presumes to delay them by the punctilious announcements which a younger butler might have considered indispensable.

My aunt was seated upon the smaller of the two settees, in the attitude of dignified expectation which I had come, through long acquaintance, to recognise as the precursor to a communication of some importance; her hands, gloved still, were folded in her lap; her eyes, behind the small round spectacles which she affected only at home and never in company, were fixed upon the fender, where the brass had been so meticulously polished that the small flames repeated themselves, in miniature and inverted, upon its surface.

My cousin Mildred, whom I had not, in honesty, expected to find at home in such weather and at such an hour, occupied the further window-seat with that absolute stillness of which only the truly unhappy are, in my experience, capable; and the new young curate, Mr. Pelham, whose engagement to Mildred had been, until that morning, the principal cheerful subject of conversation in the parish, was nowhere in evidence.

Aunt Cordelia turned her head, with that slow deliberate motion which preceded all her significant announcements, and indicated, by the slightest possible gesture of one gloved hand, the chair which had been placed, evidently for me, before the fire.

"Sit down, James," she said. "There has been an unpleasantness."

I sat down.

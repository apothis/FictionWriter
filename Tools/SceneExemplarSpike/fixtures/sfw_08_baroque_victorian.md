The letter, when at last it arrived — for it had been promised, in the most equivocal of terms, by the autumn post, and the autumn had now exhausted itself into a December so unrelenting in its grey that the very pavements of the square seemed each morning to have been laid down anew in slate — was delivered to me by Mrs. Halford herself, who, as was her custom in matters which she considered to be of some delicacy, declined to send it up by Mary, but came up the stairs in person with the salver in her hand and a face composed into that careful arrangement of features by which the truly perceptive of her class express, without descending into vulgar curiosity, a complete and exact knowledge of the situation upon which they have been called to attend.

She set the salver upon the small table beside the window. She did not, I thought, look at me directly; she looked at a point upon the wallpaper a foot or so to the left of my head, where, by the curious arrangement of the pattern, a small green bird appeared to be eternally upon the point of taking flight.

"From the Continent, ma'am," she said.

"Yes," I said. "Thank you, Mrs. Halford."

She did not leave. She busied herself, for a moment, with the curtain, which was already drawn open, but which she now drew somewhat more open, and then, with the species of slow deliberation which I had come to recognise as her particular mode of granting me my privacy, she descended the stairs.

I waited until the sound of her footfall had reached the lowest landing before I touched the envelope. I did not, immediately, open it. I looked at the hand. The hand was his. It had been so long since I had seen the hand that I had begun, in certain weak moments which I do not now wish to recount, to doubt whether I could any longer have identified it; but I knew it instantly. It is a curious thing how the hand of one whom one has loved survives, in the deeper register of the memory, all the small ordinary forgettings of the surface.

I opened the letter.

The cathedral was older than the city and stood at the centre of it the way a stone stands at the centre of a riverbed, with the water of the streets running around it on both sides and dividing again at the far end and rejoining beyond the apse where the old graveyard had once been. Iliana had seen pictures of it all her life and she had thought she knew, from the pictures, what it would be. The pictures had been wrong. The pictures had shown the front, which was the least interesting part of the building — a flat, ornate facade of saints and martyrs in their niches, sun-faded by six centuries of southern light, the kind of stonework you stopped seeing after the first photograph of it. The sides were different. The sides were what nobody photographed.

She walked up the north side because that was the side away from the square, away from the bells and the pigeons and the loose dogs of the morning market. On this side the wall fell straight to the cobble for sixty feet without a single decoration, no statue, no flourish, no inset niche, just blank weathered limestone darkening at the base where the rain had run down it for centuries and stained the stone in a long uneven hem. Above her, very far above, the windows began. They were the deep lancet windows of the early gothic, narrow as wrists, set so high in the wall that even from across the lane she could not see them properly, could only see the lead between the panes and the suggestion of colour where the southern light, pushing through from the far side, made certain panes glow faintly from within the dimness of the church interior — a red here, a blue there, a green so dark it might have been black, all of it too high to read as figures or stories, all of it just bright wounds in the wall.

The buttresses began halfway down. They came out of the wall in pairs, like the arms of swimmers reaching, and met the ground in solid wedges of stone that were dirty with rain and pigeon-streak and the slow patient lichen of a wet country. Between the buttresses the wall went into deep cold pockets of shadow that the sun would not reach until late in the afternoon, and Iliana, walking between them, felt the temperature of each pocket drop noticeably as she crossed it, as if she were walking through a series of small refrigerated rooms, each one cooler than the lane.

At the far end she came to the door. It was the side door of the cathedral, the one used for the priest's house and for funerals; the tourist door was on the front. The door was smaller than she had expected. It was a single low door of black-iron-banded oak, set into the wall under a plain stone arch with no carving on it at all. The iron was rusted. The wood was dark with years of hands.

She put her hand flat against it, just to feel the cold of it.

The door, when she pushed it, opened.

The kitchen was the coldest room. Sela came down at four because she had stopped pretending to sleep, and she sat at the table without turning on a light, and she watched the window go from black to grey to that thin colourless dawn she had once thought was hopeful and now thought was nothing.

She had read somewhere that grief had stages. She had thought about this, often, in the early months, because she was the kind of person who liked a map. She had wanted to know where she was, on the map, and how far to the edge. She wanted a chart she could circle her position on with a pencil, the way her father had circled their position on weather charts in the boat when she was small, before she had ever lost anyone. The stages were supposed to be: denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance. She had decided, eventually, that whoever had drawn that map had never been here. Here was not a sequence. Here was a room. You could be in denial in the morning and in acceptance by lunch and in nothing at all by three. You could feel, on a Tuesday at five-fifteen, exactly what you had felt on the very first day, with no warning, with no fading at the edges, sharp and whole and complete, as if the months between had not occurred. The stages, she had decided, were a story that people told the people watching them. They were a politeness.

She thought, sometimes, that she had been generous to him. She did not let herself think this often because it was the wrong kind of thought and it had a feeling like rot at the centre of it. But it came back. She had been generous. She had taken nothing of what he had been, not really, not the bad parts; she had not asked the questions that would have made the rest of the marriage harder. She had let him have whatever he wanted to have, including, in the end, his version of how he died, which was not the true version. She had let him die well in everyone's memory. She had done that for him. He had not asked her to and she did not expect a thank-you, because he could not give one now, but she had done it, and on bad mornings, like this one, she let herself notice that she had done it.

The kettle clicked. She had not heard herself fill it.

She poured the water over the tea bag and watched the colour come up out of it in a slow brown ribbon. There was nothing she had to do today. There was nothing she had to do this week. The light at the window had turned, while she was thinking, into the kind of light that meant morning had started, properly, and she would have to find a way to be inside it.

She drank the tea. It was too hot. She drank it anyway. The pain of it was a thing that was happening right now and not anywhere else, and she was grateful for it the way you are grateful for a noise that drowns out a different noise.

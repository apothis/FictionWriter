For three years after she left, nothing happened.

This is not quite true. Things happened to Hadley the way things happen to anyone — she rented an apartment, she lost the apartment, she rented a smaller apartment, she got a job at a bookshop and quit it and got a job at a different bookshop and kept it. She made one friend who was good and three friends who were not. She read a great deal. She wrote one short story that was published in a small magazine and four short stories that were not published anywhere. She had two relationships, neither of them serious. She did not call her mother. Her mother did not call her. She did not go home for Christmas, or Christmas after that, or Christmas after that. None of this counts as anything happening. This is just the shape of three years passing.

In the fourth year, her father died.

She got the message on a Tuesday afternoon, standing in the children's section of the bookshop where she worked, shelving a stack of picture books in alphabetical order by author. She put the books down. She went out the back of the shop into the alley and stood with her hand flat on the bricks for a long time. Then she went back in and finished her shift because she did not know what else to do, and because the manager was a woman who had a complicated relationship with grief and Hadley did not want to test it on a Tuesday.

She flew home on the Friday.

The house was unchanged. The house had been unchanged for forty years and it would, presumably, go on being unchanged after her mother also died, though she did not say this out loud at the funeral or after. Her mother had aged, in three years, more than three years; the difference was distinct. There was a softness around the mouth that had not been there. There was a slowness in getting up from a chair.

"I'm sorry I didn't come back," Hadley said, on the second evening, at the kitchen table, after the food was done and only the candles were left. "I should have come back."

"I know."

"I don't have a good reason."

"I know that too."

"Why didn't you call me."

Her mother thought about this. She thought about it for what felt to Hadley like a long time. Then she said: "I was angry."

"At me?"

"Yes."

"For?"

"For going. For not telling me where. For making me find out where, from your aunt, six months later. For all of it."

Hadley nodded.

"And then you didn't call me back. So I was angry about that too."

"Okay."

"I'm too old to be angry anymore," her mother said. "I am not going to be angry about it anymore starting now. But I want you to know it was there. I want you to know I had it."

"Okay."

They sat there. The candles burned down to where the holders were.

Hadley stayed for six more days. She left on the following Thursday. She came back two months later, and again a month after that, and then once a month, and then more, and her mother lived for another eleven years, and in those eleven years they talked about everything except the three years of silence, which they never talked about again, by mutual agreement they had never spoken aloud and never needed to.

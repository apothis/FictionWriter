Mara stood in the doorway and did not move.

"You said you weren't coming back," Daniel said.

"I lied."

He held the door but did not invite her in. The hallway behind her smelled like rain.

"Why."

"You know why."

"I really don't."

She lifted the bag she was carrying — a small canvas bag, not the kind for travel — and set it on the floor between them. He looked at it. Then he looked at her.

"What's that."

"It's the letters. All of them. The ones you wrote me and the ones you didn't send. The drafts. The pages you tore up and the pages you folded into your books. I went through everything."

Daniel did not speak.

"You don't have to keep them," she said. "I just thought you should see them. Together. I wanted you to see what you actually wrote."

"You went into my house."

"Yes."

"While I was at work."

"Yes."

He laughed, once, without any humor in it. "That's a felony, Mara."

"I know."

"You're standing in my doorway right now, at nine at night, and you came here to tell me you broke into my house."

"I didn't break anything. I had a key."

"I asked for the key back."

"You did."

"In March."

"In March."

He stepped back. She thought for one second that he was going to let her in. He was not. He was just stepping back to look at her better, the way a man looks at a thing he has decided to throw out.

"Pick up the bag," he said.

"No."

"Pick up the bag and take it home with you. Burn it. I don't care. I don't want it here."

"I'm not taking it home."

"Then I'll burn it."

"Good. You should. But I want you to read one first."

"No."

"One. Just one. The one on top."

He did not move. The bag sat between them, small and very still, like something that might wake up.

"Mara. Go home."

"Just the one."

He looked down at the bag for a long time. Then he crouched, the way a tall man crouches when he hasn't done it in a while, slowly and with one hand on the doorframe. He opened the bag. He took out the top envelope. He stood up.

"Read it out loud," she said.

"I'm not doing that."

"Read it."

He opened the envelope. She watched his face, not his hands. She watched him read it the way she had watched him read everything, for sixteen years.

When he was done he folded the page back in half and put it back in the envelope and put the envelope back in the bag and zipped the bag closed.

"Get out," he said.

She got out.
